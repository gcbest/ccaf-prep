The shape of the system

One Claude call is no longer enough. You are the architect, and today the question stops being “what do I send?” and becomes “what kind of system am I building?”

8:10 a.m. · The whiteboard nobody erased

One call is not enough anymore

Northstar’s returns desk has outgrown a single request. A customer sends a photo, a receipt, a sentence of frustration, and a question. The answer needs a policy lookup, a warranty calculation, a tone that survives legal review, and — sometimes — a human.

Three people are waiting for you at the whiteboard.

“Give Claude the tools and a goal and let it cook.” — Rosa Delgado, platform lead. Rosa wants everything agentic.

“Every unpredictable step is an unpredictable invoice and an unpredictable incident.” — Callum Beckett, reliability and cost. Callum wants everything deterministic.

“I just need the same customer to get the same answer twice.” — Ines Okafor, who runs the returns desk and owns the outcome.

Toby Kerr, the engineer who will actually build it, has a marker in his hand and no idea what to draw.

Everything below is about that drawing. Two shapes are available — a workflow and an agent — and four named patterns for building the first one.

Assumed from parts 1 and 2: you already know how a request is made and how tools are defined, and you know the agent loop — send a message with tools, get back either an answer or a tool-use request, execute the tool, return a tool_result, repeat until stop_reason is end_turn. This chapter is about the architecture wrapped around that loop.

First principle: orchestration is not a bigger prompt. It is a decision about how much of the control flow you write and how much you delegate.

8:34 a.m. · The two shapes

Workflow or agent?

You draw the definitions before anyone argues.

A workflow is a predefined series of Claude calls that solves a known problem. You can picture the exact steps ahead of time, so you write them as code.

An agent is given a goal and a set of tools and figures out its own path. You reach for it when you don’t know exactly what task or parameters will come in.

BenefitsDownsides
WorkflowsHigher accuracy (focus per subtask), easier to test and evaluate because the steps are known, more predictable, better for well-defined problemsLess flexible, more constrained UX, more upfront design work
AgentsFlexible UX, can combine tools in unexpected ways, handles novel situations, can ask for more inputLower success rate, harder to test and instrument because the steps are unknown ahead of time, less predictable

Ines describes the first job: every returns email must be turned into a customer reply through the same four moves — read the policy, compute the warranty window, draft the reply, check it against tone rules. Every email. Every time.

Your call

Correct. You can picture the exact steps ahead of time, and they are the same steps every time. That is the definition of a workflow: a predefined series of Claude calls for a known problem. You get higher accuracy per subtask and a system you can actually test.
Over-built. An agent is for when you don’t know what will come in or which steps are needed. Here the four moves are fixed, so handing Claude a goal and a toolbox trades away predictability and testability for flexibility you don’t need.
Backwards. The general recommendation runs the other way: prefer workflows where possible and only reach for agents when truly required. Starting agentic on a known problem means starting with the lower success rate and the harder-to-test design.

The recommendation is not neutral. Prefer workflows wherever possible; reach for agents only when truly required. Users want a product that works, not a fancy agent.

The exam’s decision rule.Steps knowable in advance → workflow (then pick the pattern). Steps genuinely unknowable, model must improvise across a flexible toolset → agent.

9:05 a.m. · Rosa’s counterexample

“Then explain this one to me”

Rosa reads out a real message from the queue: “I bought something from you people at some point last year, it broke, do I still have coverage and can you also tell me if the replacement ships to Malta?”

There is no purchase date. There is no product name. There may or may not be a shipping question. Toby cannot draw a fixed set of boxes for it, because he does not know how many boxes there will be.

Your call

Yes. The steps are not knowable in advance, and the system must be able to recognise that it is missing required information and ask for it — “when did you purchase this?” — before it can calculate anything. That is agent behaviour: goal plus tools, path chosen at runtime.
More steps is not the same as the right shape. A longer chain still assumes you know which steps run and in what order. Here the number and order of steps depend on what the customer turns out to have asked. That is the agent condition.
This is the trap the decision rule exists to prevent. Enumerating every branch is how a workflow quietly becomes unmaintainable. When you cannot enumerate the cases, stop enumerating — give Claude a goal and a small set of combinable tools instead.

Read the scenario for one thing: can you list the steps before the input arrives? Yes → workflow. No, and the model needs to choose tools and next steps dynamically → agent.

9:41 a.m. · Pattern one — Chaining

The prompt that ignores half its own rules

Toby’s launch-announcement prompt has eleven constraints: no emoji, no exclamation marks, under 120 words, mention the warranty, don’t mention competitors, professional tone, and six more. Claude honours most of them and quietly drops two or three. Repeating the rules louder does not help.

This is the long prompt problem, and chaining (also called prompt chaining) is the pattern that solves it: break a large task into smaller sequential subtasks that build on each other.

input ──▶ [ step 1: generate ] ──▶ [ step 2: focused revision ] ──▶ output
              (imperfect)              (fix only the named misses)

Step one generates the content and is allowed to be imperfect. Step two is a focused revision pass that targets specific fixes only. Each call juggles fewer constraints, so each call gets more of them right.

Toby asks which fix to try first

That is chaining. The two-step fix is exactly the remedy the pattern prescribes: accept an imperfect first draft, then send a second, narrow request that addresses only the constraints that were violated.
Already tried, already failed. Repetition inside one long prompt is the known failure mode — Claude keeps partially ignoring constraints no matter how many times they appear. Split the work into sequential steps instead.
Wrong shape. You know the exact steps: generate, then revise. That is a workflow, and specifically a chain. Introducing autonomy here buys unpredictability on a problem you had fully specified.
Wrong pattern. Parallelization is for independent evaluations that get aggregated. These constraints all apply to one evolving draft, so the steps must build on each other — that is chaining.

Chaining: sequential subtasks that build on each other. Reach for it whenever one prompt is being asked to satisfy more rules than it can hold at once.

10:20 a.m. · Pattern two — Parallelization

Four analyses, one decision

Northstar’s hardware team must choose a housing material: metal, polymer, ceramic, or composite. One giant prompt asking Claude to weigh all four against cost, weight, thermal tolerance and moulding complexity produces mush — every consideration dilutes every other.

Parallelization splits one complex task into multiple independent sub-tasks, runs them simultaneously, and then aggregates the results into a final decision.

              ┌─▶ [ evaluate metal     ] ─┐
              ├─▶ [ evaluate polymer   ] ─┤
input ────────┤                          ├──▶ [ aggregator ] ──▶ decision
              ├─▶ [ evaluate ceramic   ] ─┤
              └─▶ [ evaluate composite ] ─┘

The sub-tasks do not have to be identical — they only have to be meaningfully independent. The payoff is focus (each call analyses one thing thoroughly), modularity (each sub-prompt can be improved and evaluated on its own), and scalability (adding a fifth material does not disturb the first four).

Callum asks what makes this pattern legal here

Exactly. Independence is the entry condition. Each material can be judged without knowing the verdict on the others, so the four evaluations can run simultaneously and an aggregation step compares them at the end.
Not the reason. Speed is a side effect, not the criterion — and it disappears the moment one sub-task needs another’s output. Parallelization applies when the sub-tasks are genuinely independent; the aggregator is what turns them back into one decision.
Not required. The sub-tasks don’t need to be identical. You could evaluate cost in one branch and thermal tolerance in another. What matters is that they are independent and that something aggregates them.

Parallelization: independent sub-tasks run simultaneously, then aggregated. Signature words in a scenario: “break this decision into separate evaluations and compare.”

11:08 a.m. · Pattern three — Routing

One inbox, three very different jobs

Ines’s queue contains billing disputes, outage reports, and how-do-I questions. A single prompt tuned to be gentle with billing disputes is the wrong voice for an outage, and a prompt tuned for outages is useless on a how-to.

Routing categorizes the input first — with its own Claude call — and then forwards it to one specialized pipeline built for that category.

                          ┌─▶ [ billing pipeline ]
input ──▶ [ categorize ] ─┼─▶ [ outage pipeline  ] ──▶ output
                          └─▶ [ how-to pipeline  ]

      the input travels down exactly ONE branch

That “exactly one” is the whole point. Because nothing else flows through it, each pipeline can be deeply optimized for its case — its own prompt, its own tools, its own tone.

Routing has a second, cheaper payoff. The same shape lets a production application send each task type to the cheapest model that meets its quality bar — classify with Haiku, draft with Sonnet, escalate only the genuinely hard reasoning to Opus — inside one endpoint. Routing is a quality pattern and a cost pattern at the same time.

Rosa wants to know how routing differs from parallelization

That’s the distinction. Routing categorizes, then forwards to a single specialized pipeline. Parallelization deliberately runs several sub-tasks and aggregates them. One picks a lane; the other opens every lane and merges at the end.
Not quite. Ordering is what separates chaining from parallelization. Routing’s defining move is the categorization step followed by forwarding to exactly one pipeline — the other branches never run at all.
No. Both patterns are made of Claude calls and can use tools or not. The difference is topological: routing narrows to one branch, parallelization fans out to many and aggregates.

Routing: categorize, then forward to the one pipeline built for that category. Signature words: “different kinds of request need different handling.”

11:52 a.m. · Pattern four — Evaluator-Optimizer

The part that grades its own homework

Northstar’s design team has a stranger job: a customer photographs a broken bracket and wants a replacement part modelled. The steps are fully known — describe the image in detail, model the object with a CAD library from that description, render the model, compare the render against the original photo, and if it is wrong, go back and fix it.

That final loop is the Evaluator-Optimizer pattern: a producer creates output, a grader evaluates it against criteria, and if the output is rejected, the feedback loops back to the producer for revision — repeating until it is accepted.

input ──▶ [ producer ] ──▶ [ grader ] ──▶ accepted ──▶ output
               ▲                │
               └─── feedback ◀──┘  (rejected)

Callum asks what this system is, architecturally

Right on both counts. The steps are fully known in advance, so it is a workflow; and its distinguishing feature is the producer → grader → feedback loop, so the pattern is Evaluator-Optimizer. A loop does not make something an agent.
The loop is a red herring. What makes something an agent is that Claude chooses the path. Here you wrote the path — describe, model, render, grade, revise — and the loop is a fixed part of it. This is a workflow using Evaluator-Optimizer.
Half right, and the half you missed is the point. Yes the steps are sequential, but chaining has no grader and no feedback edge. The named pattern for producer → grader → revise-until-accepted is Evaluator-Optimizer.

Evaluator-Optimizer: producer, grader, feedback, repeat until accepted. Signature words: “check the output against criteria and try again if it doesn’t pass.”

12:30 p.m. · The four patterns on one board

Toby draws them side by side

PatternShapeUse when
ParallelizationFan out to independent sub-tasks, then aggregateA decision breaks into meaningfully independent evaluations
ChainingSequential subtasks that build on each otherOne prompt carries too many constraints and drops some
RoutingCategorize, then forward to exactly one specialized pipelineDifferent input categories need genuinely different handling

Ines describes a new job to test the board: incoming supplier contracts must be scanned for risk. Each contract is checked for payment-terms risk, liability risk, and termination risk — three separate assessments, no one of which depends on the others — and then a final call weighs all three into a single go/no-go recommendation.

Which pattern is that?

Correct. Three independent assessments run simultaneously and a final step aggregates them into one decision. That is the parallelization signature: fan out, then aggregate.
No — nothing here builds on anything. Chaining is for sequential subtasks where step two consumes step one’s output. The liability assessment does not need the payment-terms assessment. Fan out and aggregate: parallelization.
No categorization step, and no single lane. Routing would send each contract to one pipeline. Here all three assessments run on every contract, and their results are combined. That is parallelization.
There is no grader and no revision loop. The final call aggregates three findings; it does not reject the output and send it back for another pass. That would be Evaluator-Optimizer. This is parallelization.

Exam habit: read the scenario for topology, not vocabulary. Sequential-and-dependent = chaining. Fan-out-and-aggregate = parallelization. Categorize-then-one-lane = routing. Grade-and-revise = Evaluator-Optimizer.

1:35 p.m. · Agents and tools

What you actually hand an agent

Rosa finally gets her agent — for the open-ended returns queue from this morning. An agent is a goal plus a tool set. Claude figures out the combination of tool calls itself, including recognising when it needs more information from the user and asking for it, the way it asks for a purchase date before it can compute a 90-day warranty expiry.

Toby’s instinct is to build one tool per scenario. You stop him. The design principle is to keep tools abstract and combinable, not hyper-specialized — Claude Code’s own toolset is generic (bash, read, write, edit, glob, grep), not refactor_code and install_dependencies. Generic primitives let Claude combine tools creatively to handle situations you never explicitly planned for.

Concretely: get_current_datetime, add_duration_to_datetime and set_reminder can be recombined into a dozen different time-related tasks. calculate_90_day_warranty_expiry can do exactly one.

Toby proposes a toolset. Which one do you approve?

Approved. A small set of abstract, combinable tools is what makes an agent useful — Claude pieces them together for cases you never enumerated, which is the only reason you chose an agent over a workflow in the first place.
You’ve rebuilt the workflow inside the agent. Twenty hyper-specialized tools means the agent can only handle the twenty situations you already thought of — you paid the agent’s reliability cost and got none of its flexibility.
Nothing left to orchestrate. A single do-everything tool gives Claude no combinations to discover and hides the failure inside opaque code. Agents work best with a small set of flexible primitives.

Agent = goal + tools. Design the tools to be pieced together, and expect the agent to ask for missing input rather than guess.

2:14 p.m. · Environment inspection

The agent that thought it had saved the file

Toby’s first agent run ends triumphantly and changes nothing. It “edited” a config file three times against content that had already moved, then reported success.

The missing discipline has a name: environment inspection. Before acting, look; after acting, look again. Claude can only perceive its environment through tool results, so the loop must explicitly feed it fresh state instead of assuming an action landed as expected.

  • Read a file before editing it, so the edit targets current content rather than a stale assumption.
  • List a directory before deciding whether to create a file in it.
  • Take a fresh screenshot after a Computer Use click or keystroke to confirm the action actually worked.

Skipping this step is a common source of agent failure. The agent flies blind and repeats or compounds an action that silently failed.

Ines’s video agent posts clips that are sometimes silent. What do you add?

That’s environment inspection. Give the agent a way to observe the result of its own action — screenshots at intervals, timestamped captions to verify dialogue placement — so it can validate the render meets expectations before the irreversible step. The reference build wires this up with off-the-shelf tools rather than anything bespoke: Whisper CPP generates the timestamped captions, FFmpeg extracts the frames. Neither is an AI trick — they're the same tools a human editor would reach for; the agent just gets to call them itself.
Retrying blindness produces more blindness. The agent never learned that the first attempt was wrong. Without a feedback mechanism that reveals the new environment state, a retry just repeats the same unverified action.
An instruction is not a perception. Claude cannot check the audio by being told to care about it; it can only perceive through tool results. Give it a tool that returns the actual state of the rendered file.

Environment inspection: look before you act, look again after. It is how an agent gauges progress, detects errors, and adapts instead of operating blindly.

Autonomy is a purchase, not a gift.You pay for it in predictability, testability, and success rate — so buy only as much as the problem requires.

3:00 p.m. · [Bonus] Managed agents

“Who runs the loop at three in the morning?”

Callum’s question is operational. Toby’s agent loop lives in a Python process on one server. If that process dies eight minutes into a forty-minute job, the job is gone.

Managed agents are a suite of APIs for building and deploying agents at scale, hosted on Anthropic’s infrastructure instead of your own server. You define an agent — tools, persona, capabilities — configure a sandbox environment, and fire off sessions from your app. Claude runs the agent loop inside an isolated container with file system access, bash execution and web search, and you stream events back out. It is enabled by default for every API account — nothing to request.

The four primitives, in order

#PrimitiveWhat it is
1AgentThe persona: model, system prompt, toolset. Reusable across many runs.
2EnvironmentWhere the agent runs: cloud or local, plus networking config.
3SessionA single run of an agent inside an environment. The unit of work.
4EventsThe messages flowing in and out: actions, tool calls, results, replies.

Your app talks to a session, the session drives work inside the environment, and everything comes back out through the event stream. You send events in and read events out — not a manual while loop.

Memory hook for the order: stage a play. Hire the actor (Agent), build the set (Environment), open a performance (Session), then watch the cue stream (Events).

3:38 p.m. · The smallest managed agent

Five steps, and one of them is an ordering trap

Toby builds the minimum viable version while everyone watches.

  1. Create the agent — name, model, system prompt, and tools; the bundled agent toolset supplies file, bash and web tools without you writing your own.
  2. Create the environment — a cloud environment with its networking configuration.
  3. Create the session — pointing at the agent id and the environment id.
  4. Open the event stream first, then send the kickoff message.
  5. Consume the stream, watching for agent.message (Claude’s text), agent.tool_use (which tool it picked), and session.status_idle (the agent is done).
agent   = client.beta.agents.create(name=..., model=..., system=...,
                                    tools=[{"type": "agent_toolset_..."}])

env     = client.beta.environments.create(
              name=..., config={"type": "cloud",
                                "networking": {"type": "unrestricted"}})

session = client.beta.sessions.create(agent=agent.id,
                                      environment_id=env.id, title=...)

# 4. open the stream BEFORE the kickoff message
# 5. read events: agent.message / agent.tool_use / session.status_idle

Toby sends the kickoff message and then opens the stream. What happens?

Correct, and this is a favourite exam detail. The stream only delivers events that occur after it opens. Open it first, then send the kickoff message, or the opening moves of the session vanish.
No replay. Events that happened before the stream opened are not delivered to it. That is precisely why the documented order is: open the stream, then kick off the work.
It doesn’t protect you. The work proceeds; you simply don’t see the beginning of it. Turn the camera on before the race starts.

The five steps: agent → environment → session → open stream, then kick off → consume events. The ordering inside step four is the one people get wrong.

4:05 p.m. · The building blocks Callum cares about

What turns a demo into an operation

Managed agents ship with the pieces that make long-running work survivable:

  • Rubrics and graders — define success criteria; a separate grader, with its own context window, evaluates the output against the rubric, and Claude iterates until it passes.
  • Memory — the agent reads a memory store before starting and writes to it when done, so runs build on each other instead of repeating themselves.
  • Multi-agent coordination — a coordinator agent delegates to specialist agents, each running in its own context window on a shared file system, and then synthesizes their findings.
  • Permissions policies — sensitive actions pause for human approval before executing.
  • Parallel sessions — many tickets or tasks at once, each in its own container.

Ines raises the one that scares her: the agent will eventually want to post a refund decision into the customer’s Slack channel.

Your call

That’s the named mechanism. Permissions policies exist exactly for this: a sensitive action pauses for human approval before it executes, so the agent keeps its autonomy everywhere else.
Grading is after the fact. A rubric and grader improve quality by making the agent revise, but they don’t stand between the agent and an irreversible side effect. For consequential actions you want an approval gate — a permissions policy.
Safe, but you threw away the automation. The building block for “powerful but supervised” already exists: a permissions policy pauses the sensitive action for approval instead of deleting the capability.

Rubrics for quality, memory for continuity, coordination for scale, permissions for consequence. Those four are what separate a managed agent from a clever script.

4:40 p.m. · Managed agents vs. a manual loop

Callum wants a rule he can put in a doc

Reach forWhen
Managed agentsThe loop would run too long (minutes to hours), touch too many files or tools, or needs to survive a network hiccup — that is, needs resumability. Caching and compaction ship on by default.
A manual loopYou want full control over the loop, the tools, and the message array.

Two jobs land on the desk at once. Job A: classify 400 support emails into three buckets, one short call each. Job B: audit an entire supplier document archive overnight — hundreds of files, bash, web lookups, roughly two hours of work that must not be lost if the connection drops.

Which goes where?

Correct. Job A is short, uniform and known — it doesn’t even need an agent, let alone hosting. Job B hits all three managed-agent criteria at once: long-running, many files and tools, and a hard requirement for resumability.
Over-applied. Managed agents earn their keep on long, tool-heavy, interruption-prone work. A 400-item classification is a quick walk around the block — running it as hosted sessions adds machinery without buying anything.
You’ll lose job B. A two-hour, many-file run that must survive a network hiccup is precisely the case where a hand-written loop becomes a liability. Keep the manual loop for when you genuinely need full control.

Memory hook: managed agents for the long expedition where the guide may be interrupted; a manual loop for the quick walk you want to steer yourself.

5:15 p.m. · The price of autonomy

What Northstar owes the system before it lets go of the wheel

Rosa asks for one more increment of autonomy on the returns agent. You say yes — on conditions, and the conditions are the same for a workflow or an agent.

Before you widen the leash, the design must define:

  • Stop conditions — what “done” means, so the loop ends deliberately rather than by exhaustion.
  • Timeouts — a bound on how long the system may run.
  • Permissions — what it is allowed to touch.
  • Observability — the ability to see what it did and why, which is exactly what an agent costs you by default, since the steps aren’t known ahead of time.
  • A human approval path for consequential side effects.

Then the trade-offs, stated plainly. More autonomy means lower success rates and less predictability; it means testing gets harder because you cannot enumerate the execution path; and it means cost and latency become variable, because you no longer control how many turns the system takes. A workflow’s bill is roughly knowable in advance. An agent’s is a distribution.

Callum will sign off on exactly one condition first. Which do you insist on?

That’s the list. For either design — workflow or agent — define stop conditions, timeouts, permissions, observability, and a human approval path for consequential side effects. Autonomy without those is just an unbounded process with a good vocabulary.
Capability is not control. A stronger model may reason better, but it does not give you a stop condition, a timeout, a permission boundary, or a trace of what happened. It raises cost while leaving the governance gap exactly where it was.
Instructions are probabilistic. “Be careful” has a non-zero failure rate on every run. The guarantees you need — bounded runtime, scoped permissions, an approval gate — have to be enforced by the system around the loop, not requested inside it.
Useful, but not the prerequisite. Caching reduces the cost of the tokens you send; it does nothing about how many turns an autonomous loop decides to take, what it is allowed to touch, or whether you can see what it did.

The architect’s bargain: every step of autonomy you grant, you pay for in predictability and testability, and you must buy back in stop conditions, scope, and visibility.

5:50 p.m. · The board at the end of the day

What Toby finally drew

Northstar’s returns system ends the day as a mixture, and that is the correct answer.

Inbound messages hit a routing step that sorts them into billing, outage, and how-to lanes. The billing lane is a chain: draft, then a focused revision pass against the tone rules. Contract review runs as parallelization with an aggregator. The replacement-part pipeline is an Evaluator-Optimizer loop. And the one genuinely open-ended lane — messages with missing information and unpredictable follow-ups — is an agent with four abstract tools, environment inspection built into every action, and a permissions policy on anything that touches a customer’s money.

The overnight archive audit runs on managed agents, because it is long, tool-heavy, and must survive an interruption.

Rosa: “So I got my agent.” Callum: “In one lane. With a leash.” Ines: “And the same customer gets the same answer twice.”

Real systems are not one shape. The architect’s job is to spend autonomy where the problem is genuinely open and spend structure everywhere else.

The architect’s pocket card
